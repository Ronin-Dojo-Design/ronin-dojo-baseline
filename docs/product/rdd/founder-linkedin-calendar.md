## Cadence recommendation

**Run this as 3 founder posts/week for 4 weeks, mid-week, mid-morning.**

- **Volume — 3×/week from Brian's personal profile.** The 2026 cadence data (#280 §1) is consistent:
  a steady 2–3 posts/week correlates with materially higher follower growth than bursts, and
  *consistency beats volume* for a solo operator. Three is the top of the "sustainable-solo" band and
  the right dose for a launch month; drop to 2×/week after week 4 if the weekly approval pass starts
  slipping. Empty slot > invented content — if a week can't clear the hard-rule checklist, ship two,
  not three.
- **Days — Tuesday / Wednesday / Thursday (the default in this file).** Mid-week is the LinkedIn
  engagement window; the launch beat leads Tuesday so the week builds on it. **Alternative spacing:**
  Monday / Wednesday / Friday if the operator prefers gaps between posts over a tight mid-week cluster
  — swap the weekday labels below, the content order is unchanged.
- **Time — one consistent mid-morning slot** (e.g. ~8–10am in Brian's timezone). Pick one and hold it;
  the algorithm and the audience both reward predictability more than a "perfect" time.
- **Company-page mirror — optional, 1×/week.** #280 §1 shows personal profiles out-engage company
  pages by a wide margin, so the personal profile is the engine. If the RDD company page is stood up,
  mirror only the single best-performing post of the week to it — don't double-produce.
- **After week 4 — loop, don't stop.** This calendar is a launch arc (site-live → build-in-the-open →
  craft/thesis → proof/what's-next). When it ends, the repeatable spine is the five pillars below:
  draft the next month from real shipped work (a launch, a build decision, a BBL surface), same
  approval gate, same 3×/week rhythm. Content is a **projection of real work-product** (#280 §3) — the
  next month writes itself out of whatever actually shipped.

### Day 3 · Wk1 · Wed — P2 · [POV] Why one kernel, not one product

**Hook:** Most software studios sell you a product. I build a kernel and sell you what it becomes.

**Full draft:**

> Here's the distinction, because it's the whole thesis.
>
> A product studio builds you an app. If you're lucky it's good. If you want something different
> next year, you're back at zero, paying full price for infrastructure you already paid for once.
>
> A kernel is different. It's the shared substrate — the auth, the admin tooling, the design system,
> the feature modules that already work — built once, hardened by being used, then reshaped per brand.
> When I stand up a new brand, I'm not starting from a blank repo. I'm skinning a foundation that's
> already been through real production use elsewhere.
>
> That's the bet behind Ronin Dojo Design: build the boring, hard infrastructure once, extremely well,
> and let every brand on top of it inherit that quality for free. The founder-led studios that scale
> past one good-looking site are the ones that figured out how to stop rebuilding the same plumbing
> every engagement.
>
> It also means when I tell you a module works, I mean it's already carrying real weight somewhere
> else — not that it passed a demo.

**CTA:** Curious what "kernel" means concretely? Next post walks through it with a real example.

### Day 9 · Wk2 · Tue — P4 · [PROCESS] What "kernel → brand → app" actually means in the repo

**Hook:** Here's the model from the last two posts, made concrete.

**Full draft:**

> Last week I talked about building one kernel instead of one product. Here's what that looks like
> day to day.
>
> The kernel is a shared UI and infrastructure layer — the components, the design tokens, the feature
> modules (things like lead intake, member directories, claim/verification workflows) that any brand
> can use. None of it is brand-specific. It doesn't know or care which brand is consuming it.
>
> A brand is a skin on top: a set of design tokens (colors, type, spacing) and business rules layered
> onto the kernel. Brands don't fork the kernel — they configure it.
>
> An app is the deploy unit: one brand, one Vercel project, one database, live at its own domain. This
> matters more than it sounds like it should. It means a bug fix or a new feature built for one brand
> is available to every brand instantly, without a merge, a rewrite, or a "let's revisit that
> eventually." It also means each brand can grow at its own pace without dragging the others along or
> being dragged by them.
>
> Most of what looks like "building a new brand fast" from the outside is actually this: almost
> everything was already built. Only the skin and the specific business rules are new.

**CTA:** If you're the kind of person who wants to see the actual architecture doc behind this, say so
in the comments — happy to go deeper.

### Day 10 · Wk2 · Wed — P5 · [CASE] Inside BBL: the claim loop, not the client story

**Hook:** The interesting part of BBL isn't the martial arts. It's the verification mechanic under it.

**Full draft:**

> I said I wouldn't lead with testimonials, so here's the engineering instead.
>
> The hardest part of a lineage product isn't storing "who trained under whom" — that's a graph, and
> graphs are well understood. The hard part is trust: anyone can claim a rank or a connection. The
> system has to separate an unverified claim from a confirmed one, and it has to do that without
> turning verification into a bottleneck that kills adoption.
>
> BBL's answer is what I call the claim loop: a person claims a spot in the lineage, that claim is
> checked against real verification signals, and only verified claims render as fact in the public
> graph. Unverified claims stay visibly unverified — the system doesn't quietly upgrade trust it
> hasn't earned. That distinction — visibly-unverified versus quietly-assumed — is the actual design
> decision, and it's the one most "just build a database" versions of this idea get wrong.
>
> This is the pattern I mean when I say the graph is the asset. Not the UI, not the membership tiers —
> the verified structure underneath them, because that's what makes the whole thing worth trusting.

**CTA:** If you're building anything where trust-of-data matters more than volume-of-data, this
tradeoff is worth stealing.

**Asset needed:** Screenshot of the BBL lineage view showing a verified vs. unverified state
distinction (UI-level, no personal data).

### Day 16 · Wk3 · Tue — P7 · [PROCESS] Tokens are the contract, not the color picker

**Hook:** The most important design decision I made for this whole system has nothing to do with how
anything looks.

**Full draft:**

> Every brand on the kernel could look different because of one boring rule: components never
> reference a literal color. They reference a token — a named role like "primary" or "muted
> foreground" — and a brand is just a block of values assigned to those roles.
>
> That sounds like a small technical choice. It isn't. It's the difference between "redesign a brand"
> being an afternoon's work (swap the token values) versus a project (hunt down every hardcoded hex
> across the codebase and hope you got them all). It's also the difference between brands drifting
> apart over time and brands staying provably consistent, because they're reading from the same
> contract, not copies of a similar-looking one.
>
> The rule I hold myself to: if I'm tempted to write a literal color into a component, that's a sign
> the token system is missing a role, not a reason to skip the token system. Fix the contract, don't
> route around it.
>
> Boring infrastructure, done once, done right, is most of what "it just works across every brand"
> actually is.

**CTA:** This is the kind of decision that never shows up in a portfolio screenshot but is the reason
the portfolio is possible at all.

**Asset needed:** Screenshot of a token file, or a side-by-side of the same component rendered under
two different brand token sets (no client-identifying content).

### Day 17 · Wk3 · Wed — P8 · [POV] What would Apple do?

**Hook:** The single most useful design question I know is four words long.

**Full draft:**

> Not "what's technically possible" and not "what could we add." What would Apple do.
>
> In practice that means: one well-made foundation and a small number of single-purpose pieces, not
> five overlapping components that each almost do the job. It means ratifying a decision as law and
> then actually conforming everything to it, instead of letting three different half-decisions coexist
> because nobody wants to clean up. It means killing something that's confidently wrong the moment you
> notice it, instead of working around it forever out of politeness to old code.
>
> It's a discipline question more than a taste question. The taste part (does it look clean) is
> downstream of the discipline part (did you actually resolve the decision, or did you just add
> another option). Most software doesn't feel like Apple software because it's ugly. It feels
> undisciplined because every screen is a slightly different answer to the same question.
>
> I apply that lens to code and to process, not just UI — a doc that's confidently wrong gets fixed on
> sight, not filed away for later. Lean beats sprawling, every time, even when sprawling is faster
> this week.

**CTA:** What's the discipline question you ask before you ship something? Genuinely curious what other
builders use as their version of this.

**Asset needed:** None — a quote-card-style text post works well here if a visual is wanted.

### Day 23 · Wk4 · Tue — P10 · [PROCESS] One card, not five kinds of card

**Hook:** A small refactor that taught me more about design systems than most of the big ones did.

**Full draft:**

> Early on, this codebase had a component that had grown a "kind" prop — one card component trying to
> be five different cards depending on which string you passed it. It technically worked. It was also
> exactly the kind of thing that quietly makes a codebase worse every month, because every new use
> case gets handled by adding another branch instead of asking whether it belongs there at all.
>
> The fix wasn't "write a better card." It was deciding, as law: one shared surface primitive, and a
> small number of named, single-purpose components built on top of it, each with its own tight,
> specific shape. A listing card is not a board card is not a member card, even though they all sit on
> the same visual surface underneath.
>
> The unglamorous part is that this took longer than just adding another branch to the god-component
> would have. It always does. But it's the difference between a design system that stays legible a
> year later and one that turns into an archaeology project.
>
> Ratify the decision, then actually conform every use site to it. That second half is the part most
> teams skip.

**CTA:** If your codebase has a component quietly turning into a kind-union, that's usually worth
fixing before it's load-bearing everywhere.

**Asset needed:** Before/after-style diagram — one card component with a kind-union branch tree vs. one
surface + named components (conceptual, not a literal code screenshot with proprietary logic).
